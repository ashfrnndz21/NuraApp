# Smart nudges, the feeling cloud, and what makes it sticky

Reminders are scheduled. Gap nudges ask for facts. Smart nudges are different: they are generated from how the person is doing this week, and they exist to make the app feel like it knows him. The feeling cloud is the check-in that gives the nudges something to work with.

---

## 1. Eight kinds of smart nudge

| Kind | What it says | Generated from | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Presence** | What family did, in one line | Family thread, keys, confirmations | "Mei saw your reading this morning." |
| **Commitment** | His own words back to him, one tap | Memo cards | "You said less salt at lunch. How did today go?" |
| **Pattern** | What the system is watching, with reassurance | Correlation, trends | "Pressure higher three mornings. Not urgent. I'm watching, and Ash knows." |
| **Check-in** | The feeling cloud | State change | "New tablet this week. Are you feeling like… dizzy, cramps, thirsty." |
| **Recognition** | The number that only goes up, quietly | Confirmations, walks | "Forty-one days with every tablet taken." |
| **Anticipation** | What is coming, one day ahead | Appointments spine, reorder dates | "Dr Tan tomorrow at 10. Bring the pressure book." |
| **Curiosity** | A twenty-second explainer tied to this week | Self-search, compression | "Why the water pill makes you pass water." |
| **Context** | The world outside, only when it touches his conditions | Environmental jobs, calendar | "Haze in Air Itam. Walk indoors today." |

Rules: at most two smart nudges a day for the patient, none in quiet hours, none on a day that already carries an alert or a red flag. Ranked by State: after a change (new medicine, discharge, drift) check-in and pattern come first; in a steady week presence and recognition come first. Every nudge carries "why you're seeing this". Dismissals feed ranking.

## 2. The feeling cloud

**What it is.** A strip on Today under the one thing: "Are you feeling like…" and a handful of plain words. Never a form, never a modal, never a scale from one to ten. Tap a word and it asks one thing back ("When?" "Both legs?"), then says what it will do.

**Where the words come from.** A base set of everyday feelings, with emphasis and additions driven by State:

- Water pill started: dizzy, cramps, thirsty, passing water a lot.
- Statin started: muscle aches, tired.
- Pressure rising: headache, dizzy, blurry.
- Heart failure, home from hospital: short of breath, swollen ankles, heavier, muddled, no appetite.
- Diabetes: thirsty, numb feet, blurry, shaky and sweaty.
- Haze week: cough, eyes stinging, short of breath.

Words that matter this week are larger and appear first; the rest sit smaller behind them. "Fine today" is always present and always green.

**When it appears.** After a State change, when readings drift, on day one to seven after a discharge, when the person opens the app at an odd hour, and otherwise at most once a day. It disappears after a tap or "Fine today" and does not return until something changes.

**What a tap does.** Logged in the person's words with the time and what they took. Read against medicines, readings and what changed this week. Then one of three outcomes: logged and watched ("I'll ask again in a week"), added to the visit questions ("is the dose right?"), or escalated. Escalation is immediate for red flags: chest tightness, breathlessness at rest, one-sided swelling, worst-ever headache, sudden blurring, a fall, confusion, shaky-and-sweaty on sugar medicines, a kilo or more of weight gain in two days after a heart discharge. The wash turns to the act colour, the family is told, and the not-feeling-well flow opens.

**What it is not.** It is not a symptom checker and it never names a condition. The inference is a pattern with a reason ("dizzy on standing with the water pill is common in the first weeks") and an action. Red flags are drawn from the person's own discharge plan and conditions, with the boundary line under every inference.

**Why it makes the product smarter.** Symptoms are the signal every other layer is missing. Readings and labs say what the body is doing; the cloud says what the person is feeling, timestamped against what they took. Correlation, the pre-visit brief, and the polypharmacy review all get better with it, and the doctor gets "dizzy on standing, four mornings, since the water pill" instead of "he's been a bit off".

## 3. What makes it sticky

**For the patient**

- The morning card is a ritual tied to breakfast, not an app to open.
- Taken gives a small physical confirmation and a presence line back ("Ash can see this").
- The number that only goes up. No streak to lose.
- Recall: "when did I last see Dr Tan" answered in his language, with the clip. Nothing else does this.
- The feeling cloud is a five-second way to be heard, and something happens when he taps.
- A meal photo gets a verdict about him in seconds.
- Your numbers after every result, in one sentence he can repeat to the doctor.
- The visit companion makes the app obviously useful four times a year.
- Family in the same feed: photos and notes beside health cards.
- The weekly clip, forwardable to the group.
- The emergency card on the Lock Screen is the reason it stays installed.
- It remembers. "Last week you said…" is what makes it feel like his, not a product.
- The doctor trusts the list. External validation is the strongest retention signal there is.

**For the caregiver**

- "What changed" replaces the daily phone call and takes thirty seconds.
- Reassurance: "nothing missed this week" is a reason to open it on a good week.
- The probe: answers with provenance instead of a guess.
- Visit prep saves an hour and makes her look organised in the room.
- Reorders handled; the roster makes the load visible to the siblings.
- Presence in reverse: seeing that he tapped Taken is worth more than any dashboard.

**For the helper**

- Today's list with pictures, in her language, and "given" replies. It removes the burden of remembering, which is why she keeps using it.

**Network effect.** Every key holder adds signal (a note, a reading, a confirmation), which sharpens the nudges, which gives everyone another reason to open it. The family is the retention loop; the individual never was.

---

## 4. What is refused

Streaks that can be broken. Badges, points, leaderboards. Daily quizzes. Generic content not tied to his State. Notification volume as a growth lever. Ranking for time-in-app. Any nudge that scolds. The moment any of these appear, the caregiver reads it as nagging and uninstalls it on the patient's behalf.

---

## 5. Measures

Patient: daily Taken rate; check-in taps per week and the share that end in "Fine today"; recall uses; nudge acceptance versus dismissal by kind; format switches. Caregiver: weekly opens of "what changed"; probes per week; visit briefs used. System: red-flag escalations that reached a person within five minutes; nudges suppressed by caps (a health signal, not a failure). Retention at month six for the paying family remains the gate.
